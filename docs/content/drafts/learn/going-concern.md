# Going Concern

This lesson is part of the **Small-Cap Stocks, Float And Dilution** course. It follows [Cash Runway](/learn/cash-runway/) and leads into [Trading Plan](/learn/trading-plan/).

The goal is not to make small-cap trading look easy or exciting. The goal is to understand the company, share structure, financing context, liquidity, and risk before treating any move as meaningful.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Explain going concern language in filings.
- Review substantial doubt language, cash, losses, debt, management plans, and financing history.
- Understand why going concern risk does not guarantee bankruptcy or dilution but changes context.
- Avoid ignoring financial risk because a stock has fresh news or a strong chart.
- Connect going concern review to cash runway, dilution risk, and completed-trade review.

Going concern is accounting and filing language that can warn traders a company may not have enough financial resources to continue operating without raising more money, improving operations, or changing its financial position. Traders often review going concern language because it can point to cash runway, financing pressure, and dilution risk.

A going concern warning does not guarantee bankruptcy, dilution, delisting, or a price move. It is a risk disclosure. The filing still needs to be reviewed alongside cash, burn rate, debt, revenue, financing history, and management's plan.

## Quick Definition

Going concern language means there is substantial doubt, or a similar concern, about a company's ability to continue operating over a future period unless conditions improve.

Going concern risk may appear in:

1. Form 10-K filings.
2. Form 10-Q filings.
3. Auditor reports.
4. Financial statement notes.
5. Management discussion and analysis.
6. Risk factors.
7. Press releases.
8. Financing disclosures.

For traders, the key question is what the company says it needs to do to address the risk.

## Why It Matters To Traders

Going concern language matters because it can show a company has financial pressure. That pressure can increase the chance of future financings, debt restructuring, cost cuts, asset sales, or other corporate actions.

This is where traders can get into trouble. A stock may have strong news, but the filings may show the company has going concern risk and limited runway.

A careful review asks:

1. How much cash does the company have?
2. How much cash is it burning?
3. Does the company have debt due?
4. Is revenue growing or shrinking?
5. Does management expect to raise capital?
6. Is there an active shelf or ATM?
7. Has the company raised money recently?
8. Did the auditor include going concern language?

The warning is not the whole story, but it is important context.

## How It Works

Going concern language usually appears when there is uncertainty about a company's ability to continue operating without additional support. The reasons can include low cash, recurring losses, negative cash flow, debt obligations, or limited revenue.

A company may try to address going concern risk through:

1. Stock offerings.
2. Private placements.
3. Debt financing.
4. Cost reductions.
5. Asset sales.
6. Revenue growth.
7. Strategic partnerships.
8. Mergers or restructuring.

For traders, the issue is not just that the warning exists. The issue is whether the plan to address it is realistic.

Going-concern wording is technical. The practical filing question for traders is what conditions raised the concern, what management plans to do, and whether later filings show improvement or continued pressure.

## Example Scenario

A company reports $3 million in cash, recurring losses, and a going concern warning in its 10-Q. Two weeks later, the stock spikes on contract news.

A stronger review asks:

1. Is the contract enough to solve the cash issue?
2. Does the company still need to raise money?
3. Is there an active shelf registration?
4. Did the company use prior news spikes to finance?
5. How much runway is left?
6. Did the stock hold the news move or fade?

This helps traders separate business updates from balance sheet risk.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is assuming going concern means the company will immediately fail. It does not.

Another mistake is ignoring going concern language because the stock is moving on news.

Traders also get into trouble when they treat all going concern warnings the same. Some companies raise capital and improve. Others continue to struggle.

A fourth mistake is not checking whether the going concern warning is new or repeated from prior filings.

## Review Questions

A structured trade review can help connect going concern risk to trade decisions.

Useful review notes include:

1. Filing date.
2. Location of going concern language.
3. Cash balance.
4. Cash burn.
5. Debt or obligations.
6. Management's plan.
7. Active shelf or ATM.
8. Recent offering history.
9. Whether the warning affected your trade plan.

Over time, this can help traders avoid ignoring financial risk during strong chart moves.

## FAQ

### What does going concern mean?

Going concern language means there may be substantial doubt or uncertainty about a company's ability to continue operating without improving its financial position.

### Does going concern mean bankruptcy?

No. Going concern language does not automatically mean bankruptcy. It is a risk warning that needs context.

### Why do traders care about going concern warnings?

Traders care because going concern risk can point to cash pressure, financing needs, dilution risk, or operational weakness.

### Where is going concern language found?

It can appear in financial statement notes, auditor reports, risk factors, management discussion, and SEC filings such as 10-K or 10-Q.

### Does going concern guarantee dilution?

No. It does not guarantee dilution, but it can increase financing risk if the company needs cash.

### What should traders check with going concern language?

Traders should review cash, burn rate, debt, management's plan, offering history, active shelf registrations, and later financing activity.

## Apply This In Review

When reviewing a small-cap trade or watchlist idea connected to Going Concern, ask:

- What did I know about the company, float, share structure, and filings before the move?
- Was the move driven by news, filings, volume, social attention, sector momentum, or thin liquidity?
- Did the concept actually matter in this case, or was I forcing it onto the chart?
- What changed after the move: volume, spread, dilution risk, offering risk, or liquidity?
- Did I account for the downside scenario, or did I focus only on the exciting version of the setup?
- What would I want to notice faster the next time this situation appears?
