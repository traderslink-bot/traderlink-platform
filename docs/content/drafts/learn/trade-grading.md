# Trade Grading

This lesson is part of the **Practice And Improvement** course. It follows [Setup Screenshot Review](/learn/setup-screenshot-review/) and leads into [One-Rule Practice Drill](/learn/one-rule-practice-drill/).

Trade grading means assigning a process grade to a completed trade. The grade should measure decision quality, not just outcome.

A profitable trade can receive a weak process grade. A losing trade can receive a strong process grade. That is the point.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Grade trades by process instead of only outcome.
- Choose simple grading categories.
- Separate risk, execution, management, and behavior.
- Use grades to find repeated improvement areas.
- Avoid turning grading into self-criticism.

## Quick Definition

Trade grading is a structured review method that scores a completed trade by how well it followed the plan.

Common grading categories include:

- Setup quality.
- Risk definition.
- Entry timing.
- Position sizing.
- Execution quality.
- Trade management.
- Rule adherence.
- Review note quality.

The grade should help the trader improve, not punish the trader.

## Simple Grade Structure

Keep the first grading system simple.

Example:

- A: followed the plan with clear risk and good execution.
- B: mostly followed the plan with one manageable issue.
- C: mixed process with unclear risk or execution.
- D: significant rule break or reactive decision.
- Review only: no grade yet, more context needed.

The exact labels matter less than consistency.

## What To Grade

Grade process categories separately when possible:

- Plan: was the trade planned before the decision?
- Risk: was invalidation defined?
- Size: did size match the plan?
- Execution: did fills and timing match the idea?
- Management: were adjustments planned?
- Behavior: did emotions change the decision?

This helps avoid one vague grade that hides the real issue.

## Realistic Example

A trade closes green, but the review shows the trader entered late, used more size than planned, and exited early because the position felt uncomfortable. The outcome was positive, but the process grade may be C or D.

Another trade closes red, but the trader followed the plan, respected risk, and exited when the idea failed. That trade may receive an A or B process grade.

This keeps learning honest.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is grading only by profit and loss.

Another mistake is making the grading system too complicated.

Traders also make mistakes when they change grades based on mood.

Another mistake is using grades as shame instead of feedback.

A final mistake is grading every trade without looking for patterns across a sample.

## Practical Checklist

- The grade measures process quality.
- The grading categories are simple.
- Risk and execution are reviewed separately.
- Green trades can receive weak grades.
- Red trades can receive strong grades.
- Grades are reviewed across a sample.
- The grade leads to one improvement focus.

## Apply This In Review

- What grade did the trade earn by process?
- Which category lowered the grade?
- Did outcome bias affect the grade?
- Did I follow risk and size rules?
- Did execution match the plan?
- What grade pattern repeats across recent trades?
- What one category should I practice next?

## Trader Intelligence Bridge

Trader Intelligence is being built to help traders review completed trades by process and repeated behavior. A trade-grading habit can make those patterns easier to compare over time.

## FAQ

### What is trade grading?

Trade grading is scoring a completed trade based on process quality, such as plan, risk, execution, management, and behavior.

### Should a winning trade always get a good grade?

No. A winning trade can still have poor process.

### Should a losing trade always get a bad grade?

No. A losing trade can have strong process if the plan and risk were followed.

### How detailed should trade grading be?

Start simple. A grading system that is easy to repeat is usually more useful than one with too many categories.
