# Revenge Trading

This lesson is part of the **Trading Psychology And Discipline** course. It follows [Chasing Stocks](/learn/chasing-stocks/) and leads into [Overtrading](/learn/overtrading/).

Revenge trading happens when a trader takes another trade mainly to make back money, fix a loss, or prove they were right. It usually comes from emotion, not from a clean setup.

Every active trader knows the feeling. A loss happens, a stock moves without them, or they feel like they got shaken out. The next trade feels urgent. That urgency is often the warning sign.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define revenge trading without treating it as a character flaw.
- Identify the triggers that often happen before revenge trades.
- Review whether a trade was taken to follow a setup or to fix a feeling.
- Connect revenge trading to size, loss limits, re-entries, and overtrading.
- Build a simple interruption rule for sessions where frustration appears.

## Quick Definition

Revenge trading is emotional trading after a frustrating result.

- Re-entering too quickly after a loss.
- Increasing size to make money back.
- Taking lower-quality setups.
- Ignoring the original plan.
- Trading after hitting a daily loss limit.
- Chasing the same stock after being stopped out.
- Forcing trades because the day feels unfinished.

The problem is not one extra trade. The problem is when the next decision is driven by frustration instead of process.

## Why It Matters

Revenge trading can turn a normal planned loss into a much worse session. A planned loss is part of trading, but reacting emotionally can make the next trade larger, faster, and less structured.

- Broken daily loss limits.
- Oversized re-entries.
- Overtrading.
- Chasing after a missed move.
- Holding losers too long.
- Ignoring the original invalidation level.

This is not about being weak. It is about recognizing a pattern that can happen quickly, especially when the trader is watching fast-moving tickers or trying to recover a session.

## How It Shows Up

Revenge trading usually starts with a trigger.

- Taking a loss.
- Missing a move.
- Getting stopped out before a stock reverses.
- Watching a trade work after exiting early.
- Seeing another trader win on the same ticker.
- Wanting to get back to green on the day.
- Feeling embarrassed by a mistake.

After the trigger, the trader may start looking for a trade that fixes the feeling instead of a trade that fits the plan.

## Realistic Example

A trader loses on a morning breakout. The loss is within plan, but it feels frustrating because the stock quickly bounces after the exit.

Instead of waiting for a new setup, the trader re-enters immediately with larger size. There is no clean entry, no clear risk area, and no fresh reason for the trade. The second trade loses more than the first.

The second trade was not really about opportunity. It was about trying to erase the feeling from the first trade.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is increasing size after a loss. The trader may want to recover quickly, but larger size can make the next decision even more emotional.

Another mistake is re-entering the same ticker without a new setup. A new trade needs a new reason, not just frustration from the previous one.

Traders also make mistakes when they ignore daily loss limits because the day feels unfinished.

Another mistake is trading faster after frustration. Speed can reduce the chance that the trader checks risk, levels, spread, or setup quality.

A final mistake is treating revenge trading as something to hide. If the behavior is not recorded, it is harder to reduce.

## Practical Checklist

Before judging this behavior, check:

- Am I trying to take a setup or fix a feeling?
- Did I wait long enough to reassess clearly?
- Is this a new setup with a new reason?
- Is my size the same or smaller than planned?
- Have I hit my daily loss limit or rule-break limit?
- Would I take this trade if the previous trade had not happened?
- Do I need a break before making another decision?

## Apply This In Review

After a trade or session, ask:

- Did I take a trade mainly because I was frustrated?
- Did I increase size after a loss?
- Did I re-enter too quickly?
- Did I ignore my daily loss limit?
- Did I trade worse after my first red trade?
- Did the setup meet my normal standards?
- What was the emotional trigger?
- What interruption rule would have helped?

The key is to track the trigger. Revenge trading is easier to reduce when the pattern becomes visible.

## Trader Intelligence Bridge

Trader Intelligence is being built to help traders review behavior patterns such as trades after losses, quick re-entries, size increases, rule breaks, and emotional sequences across a session.

For revenge trading, the useful review is not whether the follow-up trade happened to work. The better review is whether the second decision was cleaner than the first or simply more emotional.

## FAQ

### What is revenge trading?

Revenge trading is taking trades emotionally after a loss, missed move, or frustrating result, usually to make money back or prove a point.

### Why do traders revenge trade?

Traders may revenge trade because losses can create frustration, urgency, fear of missing out, embarrassment, or a need to get back to green.

### Is revenge trading the same as overtrading?

They can overlap. Revenge trading is driven by emotion after a trigger, while overtrading means taking too many trades or low-quality trades.

### How can I spot revenge trading?

Look for trades taken immediately after a loss, trades with larger size than normal, trades outside the plan, or re-entries without a fresh setup.

### Can a winning trade still be revenge trading?

Yes. A revenge trade can make money and still be a process problem if the reason for the trade was frustration instead of a valid setup.

### How should revenge trading be reviewed?

Track the trigger, time between trades, size change, setup quality, whether the daily loss limit was respected, and what rule could interrupt the pattern next time.
