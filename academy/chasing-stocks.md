# Chasing Stocks

Chasing stocks means entering after a move has already become extended, usually because the trader feels pressure to get in before the move goes farther. It often overlaps with FOMO, overtrading, and emotional decision-making.

A stock can be strong and still be a bad chase. The issue is not only whether the stock is moving. The issue is whether the entry still has a clear plan, realistic risk, and enough room before the next meaningful level.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Explain what chasing means in a trade-management context.
- Separate a planned momentum entry from a late emotional entry.
- Review whether the clean risk area was already gone.
- Identify how spread, slippage, resistance, and support affect late entries.
- Create review tags for repeated chase behavior.

## Quick Definition

Chasing stocks means entering after price has already moved significantly without waiting for a planned entry, pullback, setup, or risk area.

- Entering after several large candles in one direction.
- Buying near high of day without a plan.
- Shorting after a sharp breakdown without checking support.
- Jumping into scanner alerts too late.
- Entering because chat or social media is excited.
- Ignoring spread, slippage, and nearby levels.
- Taking a trade because the trader missed the cleaner entry.

Chasing is not the same as trading momentum. Momentum trading can be planned. Chasing is usually rushed.

## Why It Matters

Chasing matters because late entries often create poor risk-to-reward. When price is extended, the risk area may be far away and normal pullbacks can feel uncomfortable.

- Poor fills.
- Wide spreads.
- Slippage.
- Entering into resistance.
- Shorting into support.
- Oversized trades.
- Revenge entries after missing a move.
- Repeated losses from low-quality setups.

The problem is not one chase. The bigger problem is when chasing becomes a repeated habit.

## How It Shows Up

Chasing often starts when a stock moves quickly and the trader feels late.

- A missed clean breakout or breakdown entry.
- A second scanner alert after the move is already extended.
- A large candle that creates urgency.
- Chat or social excitement.
- A desire to recover from missing the first move.
- A belief that no pullback will come.

Missing a move is not the same as needing to enter the next candle. A better review asks where the clean entry was, whether it was missed, and whether there is still a valid setup now.

## Realistic Example

Imagine a stock breaks above 4.00 and runs to 4.80 on heavy volume. The clean breakout entry was near 4.05, but the trader hesitated.

At 4.78, the stock hits another scanner alert. The trader enters because it feels like the stock could keep pushing. But resistance is near 4.90, the spread is wide, and the stock is already extended.

The issue is not that the stock had momentum. The issue is that the entry came after the clean risk area was gone.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is assuming that a strong stock is always worth entering. Strength matters, but entry location matters too.

Another mistake is ignoring distance from support or invalidation. If the nearest real risk area is too far away, the trade may be harder to manage.

Traders also make mistakes when they chase because they missed the first move. Missing a trade can create emotional pressure.

Another mistake is using large size on a late entry. A late entry usually deserves more caution, not more size.

A final mistake is refusing to label the trade honestly in review. If the trade was a chase, the review should say so.

## FAQ

### What does chasing stocks mean?

Chasing stocks means entering after a move is already extended, often because the trader feels pressure to avoid missing the move.

### Is chasing always bad?

Not every late entry loses, but chasing often creates weaker risk-to-reward and more emotional trade management.

### Is chasing the same as momentum trading?

No. Momentum trading can be planned with clear risk and structure. Chasing is usually rushed and emotional.

### Why do traders chase stocks?

Traders chase because of FOMO, missed entries, scanner alerts, social excitement, revenge trading, or fear that the stock will keep moving without them.

### How can I tell if I chased a trade?

Review whether the entry was late, whether risk was defined, whether price was extended, and whether you entered because of urgency rather than a plan.

### How should chasing be reviewed?

Track the original clean entry, actual entry, distance from support or resistance, emotional trigger, risk plan, and whether the trade fit your rules.
