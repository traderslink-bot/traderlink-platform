# Previous Day High Low: How Traders Review PDH and PDL

The previous day high and previous day low are the highest and lowest prices from the prior regular trading session. Traders often mark these levels before the next session because they are objective, visible reference points.

Check your chart settings before relying on them. Some platforms can include or exclude extended-hours data, which can change what appears to be the prior day's high or low.

This lesson is part of the **Chart Reading And Market Structure** learning path.


Goal: Learn how previous day high and low act as session reference levels for planning and review.

Previous day levels can help traders compare today's price action against yesterday's range. They do not guarantee a breakout, breakdown, bounce, rejection, or continuation.

## What To Know First

Before studying previous day high and low, remember:

- PDH means previous day high.
- PDL means previous day low.
- These levels usually come from the prior regular trading session.
- They are reference levels, not automatic trade signals.
- Price may break, reject, reclaim, hold, or ignore them.
- Premarket context can change how traders review the level.
- Volume, liquidity, spread, catalyst quality, and timing still matter.

The value of PDH and PDL is that they are simple and objective. The risk is treating that simplicity as certainty.

## What It Means

The previous day high is the highest price a stock reached during the prior regular trading session.

The previous day low is the lowest price a stock reached during the prior regular trading session.

Traders often abbreviate these as:

- PDH for previous day high.
- PDL for previous day low.

![Candlestick chart showing previous day high and previous day low mapped as objective reference levels.](/academy/images/chart-reading/previous-day-high-low-map.svg)

The visual above shows a simple two-session view. Yesterday's high and low become today's reference levels. The levels are useful because they are known before the current session unfolds.

## Why Previous Day Levels Matter

Previous day levels matter because they show where today's price is trading compared with yesterday's full range.

For example:

- Trading above PDH may show price is above yesterday's range.
- Rejecting near PDH may show the prior high is still a resistance area.
- Holding above PDL may show price is still inside or above yesterday's range.
- Losing PDL may show weakness compared with yesterday's range.
- Reclaiming PDL after a flush may change the review.

These are context clues. They should be combined with current volume, liquidity, spread, catalyst, and broader chart structure.

## Regular Session Versus Premarket

Most traders define previous day high and low from the prior regular session, not premarket or after-hours trading.

For consistency, decide whether your review process uses regular-session levels only or a separate extended-hours map. Mixing those definitions after the trade can make the review less useful.

That said, premarket can still matter. If a stock gaps above PDH in premarket, the open may require a different review than a stock opening quietly inside yesterday's range.

Useful planning questions include:

- Is price opening above PDH, below PDL, or inside yesterday's range?
- Did premarket already test PDH or PDL?
- Is premarket volume strong enough for those reactions to matter?
- Is the stock opening near the level or already extended away from it?
- Is there a news catalyst changing the context?

PDH and PDL are starting points for the map, not the whole plan.

## How Traders Use PDH

The previous day high can act as:

- A resistance reference area.
- A breakout reference area.
- A failed-breakout review area.
- A reclaim area if price starts below and pushes back above it.
- A retest area if price breaks above and later pulls back.

The important review question is not "did price break PDH?" It is "what happened after price interacted with PDH?"

## Failed Breakout At Previous Day High

A failed breakout at PDH happens when price pushes above the previous day high but cannot hold above it.

![Candlestick chart showing price breaking above previous day high and failing back below the level.](/academy/images/chart-reading/previous-day-high-failed-breakout.svg)

This does not mean the stock must reverse. It means the PDH interaction became a review point.

Useful questions include:

- Was PDH marked before the trade?
- Was price extended before reaching PDH?
- Did volume expand through the level or fade?
- Did price hold above PDH or quickly fall back below it?
- Did the trader treat the break as automatic continuation?
- What was the plan if PDH failed?

Failed PDH breakouts are especially important to review because obvious levels can attract crowded reactions.

## How Traders Use PDL

The previous day low can act as:

- A support reference area.
- A breakdown reference area.
- A failed-breakdown review area.
- A reclaim area if price loses PDL and then gets back above it.
- A risk reference for traders watching weak structure.

Losing PDL may show weakness, but it does not guarantee continuation. Price can flush below PDL and reclaim it quickly, especially around the open or on volatile small-cap stocks.

## Example Chart Read

A stock traded yesterday between $2.10 and $2.85. Today it opens near $2.70 and pushes toward $2.85.

A trader may mark:

- $2.85 as PDH.
- $2.10 as PDL.

If price reaches $2.85, the trader can review whether price rejects, breaks and holds, or breaks and fails back below. If price later fades toward $2.10, the trader can review whether PDL holds, breaks, or reclaims.

The levels do not predict the outcome. They create a clean map for review.

## How Traders Use Previous Day High And Low

Traders may use PDH and PDL to:

- Build a simple premarket level map.
- Compare today's price against yesterday's range.
- Review breakout and failed-breakout behavior.
- Review breakdown and failed-breakdown behavior.
- Avoid entering far from meaningful reference levels.
- Study whether obvious levels create repeated mistakes.
- Connect market open trades to objective levels.

The strongest use is preparation plus review. Mark the levels before the session, then compare actual decisions against them afterward.

## What Can Go Wrong

Previous day levels can be misused when:

- The trader assumes a break of PDH must continue.
- The trader assumes a break of PDL must keep fading.
- The trader ignores premarket context.
- The trader enters far above PDH after an extended move.
- The trader holds a failed breakout with no plan.
- The trader ignores spreads or thin liquidity near the level.
- The trader treats PDH/PDL as more important than every other level.

Obvious levels are useful, but obvious levels can also create fakeouts.

## Common Mistakes

Common mistakes around previous day high and low include:

- Not marking PDH and PDL before the session.
- Treating PDH as an automatic breakout level.
- Treating PDL as an automatic breakdown level.
- Ignoring whether price is opening inside or outside yesterday's range.
- Chasing into PDH after a large premarket move.
- Holding after a failed PDH break without reviewing the plan.
- Ignoring a PDL reclaim after weakness.
- Forgetting that a small zone around the level may matter more than the exact penny.

The level gives structure. The reaction around the level gives the review.

## What To Check On The Chart

Before using PDH or PDL in a plan or review, ask:

1. Did I mark PDH and PDL before the session?
2. Were the levels from the regular session or extended hours?
3. Is price opening above PDH, below PDL, or inside yesterday's range?
4. Did premarket already react around either level?
5. Is price near the level or extended away from it?
6. What would a hold, break, rejection, or reclaim look like?
7. Is volume meaningful around the interaction?
8. Is the spread/liquidity clean enough for the level to matter?
9. What was my plan if the level failed?
10. Did I review the actual reaction instead of assuming what the level should do?

This checklist keeps previous day levels practical and reviewable.

## Review This In Your Journal

Previous day levels are useful for review because they can be marked objectively before the session.

Ask:

- Did I know the previous day high and low before trading?
- Did I enter near PDH, near PDL, or far away from both?
- Did I chase a PDH break after price was already extended?
- Did I respect a failed PDH breakout?
- Did price lose PDL, and did it reclaim quickly?
- Did I ignore a cleaner level nearby, such as premarket high or support?
- Did the level actually matter during the session?
- Was my decision based on the level reaction or on excitement around the move?

This helps turn level trading from a vague chart idea into a reviewable process.

## Key Takeaway

Previous-day high and low are session reference points. They matter when price reacts around them with context, not simply because yesterday marked them.

## FAQ

### What does previous day high low mean?

Previous day high low refers to the highest and lowest prices from the prior regular trading session.

### Why do traders watch the previous day high?

Traders watch the previous day high because it can act as resistance, a breakout reference area, a reclaim area, or a failed-breakout review point depending on how price reacts.

### Why do traders watch the previous day low?

Traders watch the previous day low because it can act as support, a breakdown reference area, or a reclaim area after weakness.

### Does breaking the previous day high mean a stock is bullish?

Not automatically. A break above the previous day high can fail if price does not hold, volume fades, sellers step in, or the move is already extended.

### Is previous day high low useful for day trading?

Yes. Many day traders use these levels as part of premarket planning and intraday review, especially around the market open.

### Should previous day levels be exact prices?

They are objective prices, but traders often watch a small zone around them because price can react slightly above or below the exact level.

### How can I review previous day level trades?

Review whether PDH and PDL were marked before the trade, whether price held or failed the level, and whether your entry, exit, and risk decisions matched your plan.
