# Paper Trading

This lesson is part of the **Practice And Improvement** course. It follows [Practice Trading](/academy/practice-trading/) and leads into [Trade Replay Review](/academy/trade-replay-review/).

Paper trading is simulated trading. It lets a trader practice decisions without using real money.

That makes it useful, but also limited. Paper trading can help with process, order familiarity, setup recognition, and review habits. It cannot fully recreate live emotion, slippage, liquidity, or the pressure of real risk.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define paper trading.
- Understand what paper trading can teach.
- Recognize where paper trading can mislead.
- Review simulated trades as process practice.
- Decide when paper trading should be paired with other review methods.

## Quick Definition

Paper trading is trading in a simulated environment where orders, positions, and results are tracked without real capital at risk.

It can help traders practice:

- Platform navigation.
- Order entry workflow.
- Risk planning.
- Setup recognition.
- Watchlist use.
- Trade management notes.
- Post-trade review.

The key word is simulated. A paper trade is not the same as a live trade.

## What Paper Trading Can Teach

Paper trading is useful for building basic process.

- Can the trader define risk before the simulated trade?
- Can they follow a watchlist plan?
- Can they identify levels without chasing?
- Can they manage a trade according to a written plan?
- Can they review the trade honestly afterward?

These are real skills even when no money is involved.

## What Paper Trading Does Not Prove

Paper trading does not prove that a trader is ready for live size. A simulated platform may fill orders differently than the live market. The trader may feel calm because no real money is at risk. Slippage, partial fills, liquidity gaps, and hesitation can feel different live.

Paper trading is a training tool, not a guarantee.

When reviewing paper trades, record whether fills were realistic, delayed, partial, or simply assumed by the simulator. A clean simulated fill may not represent what could have happened with real orders in a fast, thin, or wide-spread market.

## Realistic Example

A trader paper trades opening range setups for two weeks. The simulated results look positive, but review shows several entries happened far from the planned risk area. In live trading, that same behavior could create bigger losses or emotional exits.

The paper trading result is less important than the process note: the trader needs to practice waiting for risk to be defined before increasing pressure.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is taking unrealistic size because the trade is simulated.

Another mistake is ignoring spread, slippage, and liquidity.

Traders also make mistakes when they skip review because no real money was involved.

Another mistake is treating a small sample of paper trades as proof.

A final mistake is paper trading in a way that does not match the trader's intended live rules.

## Practical Checklist

- The paper trade follows the same rules the trader wants to practice live.
- Position size is realistic for the future plan.
- Spread and liquidity are noted.
- Risk is defined before the simulated decision.
- The trader records why the trade was taken.
- The trade is reviewed after completion.
- Simulated results are treated as process data, not proof.

## Apply This In Review

- Did I use realistic size?
- Did I define risk before the simulated trade?
- Did I ignore fills that would be difficult live?
- Did I follow my intended rules?
- Did the platform fill unrealistically?
- What behavior would become harder with real money?
- What should I practice again before adding pressure?

## FAQ

### Is paper trading useful?

Yes, paper trading can be useful for practicing process, platform workflow, setup recognition, and review habits.

### Is paper trading the same as live trading?

No. Live trading includes real emotions, fills, slippage, liquidity, and account risk that paper trading may not recreate.

### What should I track when paper trading?

Track setup, risk, size, timing, liquidity, spread, management decisions, and whether the trade followed the practice focus.

### Can paper trading create bad habits?

Yes, if the trader uses unrealistic size, ignores execution, or treats simulated outcomes as proof.
