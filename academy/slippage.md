# Trading Slippage: How Traders Review Worse-Than-Expected Fills

Trading slippage happens when an order fills at a different price than expected. For active traders, slippage can turn a good-looking setup into a worse trade because the entry or exit is not where the plan assumed.

This lesson is part of the **Volume Liquidity And Order Flow** learning path. It builds on [Bid And Ask](/academy/bid-and-ask/), [Spread](/academy/spread/), and [Liquidity](/academy/liquidity/), then leads into [Market Orders Vs Limit Orders](/academy/market-orders-vs-limit-orders/).


## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Explain slippage in volume, liquidity, or order-flow context.
- Identify where it appears on a realistic chart or trading dashboard.
- Review the concept with price levels, execution quality, spread, liquidity, and risk in mind.
- Avoid treating it as guaranteed confirmation.

Slippage is not only a broker issue. It is often connected to liquidity, spread, order type, speed, size, and whether the trader chased a fast move.

## What You Should Understand Before Reading This

Before studying slippage, remember:

- The bid and ask show current quote availability.
- Spread can create hidden execution cost.
- Liquidity affects whether orders fill smoothly.
- Dollar volume and share volume do not guarantee clean fills.
- Order type affects speed and price control.
- Fast markets can change the quote before an order fills.

The goal is not to pretend slippage can always be eliminated. The goal is to review whether it was expected, manageable, or avoidable.

## Quick Definition

Trading slippage is the difference between the expected order price and the actual fill price.

Slippage can happen on entries and exits.

For example:

- Expected entry: `$2.00`
- Actual fill: `$2.08`
- Slippage: `$0.08` per share

![Trading dashboard showing expected price versus actual fill price.](/academy/images/chart-reading/slippage-expected-vs-actual-fill.svg)

On low-priced stocks, a few cents of slippage can meaningfully change the trade.

## Why Slippage Matters

Slippage matters because trading performance is based on actual fills, not ideal chart prices.

A trader may plan a clean risk level, but if the entry fills too high or the exit fills too low, the real risk changes. That can affect position sizing, stop distance, trade management, and review accuracy.

Slippage can affect:

- Entry quality.
- Exit quality.
- Real risk.
- Planned reward-to-risk.
- Position size.
- Whether a stop behaves as expected.
- Whether a trade should have been skipped.

This is where traders get into trouble. They review the chart as if they entered perfectly, but the execution tells a different story.

## Common Causes Of Slippage

Slippage often happens when the market moves before the order is filled or when there is not enough liquidity at the expected price.

Common causes include:

- Wide bid-ask spreads.
- Thin liquidity.
- Market orders.
- Fast price movement.
- News-driven volatility.
- Premarket or after-hours trading.
- Large order size compared with available shares.
- Stop orders triggering during a fast move.
- Volume spikes that fade quickly.

Slippage can be small and normal, or it can be large enough to change the quality of the trade.

## Slippage During Fast Moves

Fast moves can make slippage worse because quotes change quickly.

![Trading dashboard showing slippage during a fast candle with thin liquidity and a widening spread.](/academy/images/chart-reading/slippage-fast-move-liquidity-review.svg)

For example, a trader wants to enter a breakout near `$3.00`. The stock is moving quickly, the spread widens, and the order fills at `$3.12`.

Now the trade is not the same as the planned `$3.00` entry. The trader may still have had the right level, but the execution changed the risk.

The review should ask:

- Was the spread wide?
- Was the order type appropriate?
- Was there enough liquidity at the expected price?
- Did the entry fill make the risk too wide?
- Did the trader chase because the candle was moving fast?

## Entry Slippage Versus Exit Slippage

Entry slippage and exit slippage can feel different, but both matter.

Entry slippage can make the trade worse from the start. It may move the entry farther from support, resistance, or invalidation.

Exit slippage can happen when the trader tries to leave during a fast move, thin bid, wide spread, or panic candle.

Exit slippage is especially important because many traders plan risk using a stop level but forget that the actual fill may be worse than the stop trigger.

## Realistic Example

A trader plans to enter a breakout near `$2.00`. The stock has news and a volume spike. The bid is `$1.98`, the ask is `$2.08`, and the quote is moving quickly.

The trader sends an immediate order and fills at `$2.11`.

The chart review might say the breakout level was `$2.00`, but the execution review says something else:

- The entry was `$0.11` above the planned level.
- The spread was already wide.
- The order type prioritized speed over price control.
- The stop distance was larger than planned.
- The trader may have reacted to urgency instead of liquidity.

That distinction matters. The setup and execution need separate review.

## Common Mistakes

Common slippage mistakes include:

- Using immediate orders in thin stocks without checking spread.
- Ignoring spread before entry.
- Trading too much size for available liquidity.
- Assuming stop orders always fill near the stop price.
- Reviewing planned price instead of actual fill price.
- Chasing fast candles with no price control.
- Ignoring slippage when calculating risk and reward.
- Blaming every bad fill on the market without reviewing order behavior.

Slippage is often where trade planning and real execution meet.

## Practical Checklist

Use this checklist when reviewing slippage:

- What price did I expect?
- What price actually filled?
- How much slippage occurred in cents and percentage terms?
- Was the spread wide when I placed the order?
- Was liquidity thin?
- Was the stock moving quickly?
- What order type did I use?
- Was my position size too large for available depth?
- Did slippage change my planned stop or risk?
- Was the slippage normal for the conditions or avoidable?

This checklist helps keep execution review concrete.

## Apply This In Review

Slippage review should compare the planned trade with the actual fills.

Helpful review prompts:

- What was my intended entry or exit price?
- What was my actual fill?
- What caused the difference?
- Did spread, liquidity, speed, or order type contribute?
- Did I size too large for the market?
- Did slippage change the risk enough that the trade no longer matched the plan?
- Do similar trades show repeated slippage problems?
- What would I change next time: size, order type, timing, or trade selection?

Over time, this review can show whether slippage is occasional or part of a repeated execution pattern.

## FAQ

### What is trading slippage?

Trading slippage is the difference between the price a trader expected and the price where the order actually filled.

### Why does slippage happen?

Slippage can happen because of wide spreads, thin liquidity, fast price movement, order type, large position size, or volatile news-driven trading.

### Is slippage always bad?

Slippage is not always avoidable, but it should be reviewed. Small slippage may be normal in some conditions, while large slippage can change the trade's real risk.

### Can stop orders slip?

Yes. A stop order can trigger during fast movement and fill worse than the stop price if liquidity is thin or price moves quickly.

### How can traders review slippage?

Compare expected price with actual fill price, then review spread, liquidity, order type, speed, size, and whether the trade conditions made slippage likely.

### Can slippage be eliminated?

No. Slippage cannot be fully eliminated, and no order type guarantees a perfect fill. Traders can only review and manage the conditions that make slippage more likely.
