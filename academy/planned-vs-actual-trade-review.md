# Planned Vs Actual Trade Review

A planned-vs-actual trade review compares what the trader intended to do before the trade with what actually happened during the trade. This is one of the cleanest ways to find the difference between a strategy issue and a behavior issue.

The goal is not to shame the trader for every difference. The goal is to identify which differences were justified by new information and which were emotional changes made under pressure.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Explain why planned-vs-actual review is useful.
- Compare planned entry, risk, size, management, and exit with actual decisions.
- Separate valid plan adjustments from emotional rule breaks.
- Write specific review notes that can improve the next plan.
- Connect plan review to discipline and execution review.

## Quick Definition

Planned-vs-actual trade review means comparing the trade that was planned before entry with the trade that actually happened.

- Planned setup versus actual setup.
- Planned entry versus actual entry.
- Planned risk versus actual risk.
- Planned position size versus actual size.
- Planned management versus actual management.
- Planned exit versus actual exit.
- Planned review lesson versus actual pattern found.

This review shows whether the trader followed the intended process or improvised in ways that changed the risk.

## Why It Matters

Many trading mistakes are not obvious from the final result. A trade can be profitable even if the trader abandoned the plan. A trade can lose even if the trader followed the plan well.

Planned-vs-actual review helps traders avoid confusing outcome with process. It makes the trade easier to learn from because the comparison is concrete.

## Practical Review Process

Use a simple comparison format:

- Write the original setup and reason for entry.
- Write the planned entry zone and actual entry.
- Write planned risk and actual risk.
- Write planned size and actual size.
- Write planned management rules and actual adjustments.
- Write planned exit area and actual exit reason.
- Label each difference as valid adjustment, mistake, or unclear.

The label matters. Not every change is bad. The useful question is whether the change was based on new information or emotion.

## Realistic Example

A trader plans to enter near a pullback into support with risk under the support zone. During the session, the trader enters much higher because the stock starts moving quickly.

The trade later closes green, but the planned-vs-actual review shows the entry was not the planned pullback. The trader chased a faster candle, used a wider risk area, and exited early because the position felt uncomfortable.

The review lesson is not "green trade." It is "setup idea was valid, but execution did not match the plan."

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is writing the plan after the trade. That creates hindsight bias.

Another mistake is treating every difference as a failure. Sometimes new information justifies an adjustment.

Traders also make mistakes when they only compare entry and exit while ignoring size, risk, and management decisions.

Another mistake is making review notes too vague. "Did not follow plan" is less useful than naming the exact part that changed.

A final mistake is not updating the future checklist after repeated differences appear.

## FAQ

### What is planned-vs-actual trade review?

It is the process of comparing the trade plan with the decisions that actually happened during the trade.

### Why compare planned and actual trades?

It helps traders see whether results came from the intended process or from changes made during pressure.

### Are all plan changes bad?

No. Some changes are valid when new information appears. The review should identify why the change happened.

### What should be compared?

Compare setup, entry, risk, size, management, exit, and emotional behavior.

### Can a green trade still fail planned-vs-actual review?

Yes. A trade can make money while still breaking the plan.

### How detailed should this review be?

Detailed enough to identify the decision that changed and what should be clearer next time.
