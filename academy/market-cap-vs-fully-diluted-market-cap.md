# Market Cap vs Fully Diluted Market Cap

This lesson is part of the **Small-Cap Stocks, Float And Dilution** course. It follows [Fully Diluted Shares](/academy/fully-diluted-shares/) and leads into [Dilution](/academy/dilution/).

The goal is not to make small-cap trading look easy or exciting. The goal is to understand the company, share structure, financing context, liquidity, and risk before treating any move as meaningful.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Explain market cap and fully diluted market cap.
- Review how hidden future shares can change the valuation picture.
- Compare current share count with potential shares from warrants, convertibles, options, and preferred stock.
- Avoid relying on basic market cap alone when dilution context matters.
- Connect valuation context to small-cap trade review without treating valuation as a signal.

Market cap vs fully diluted market cap is an important comparison because the basic market cap may not show the full potential share count. Market cap usually uses current shares outstanding. Fully diluted market cap estimates valuation if dilutive securities become common shares.

Fully diluted market cap does not mean every possible share is currently trading. It is a risk review tool. Traders use it to understand what valuation could look like if warrants, convertibles, options, preferred stock, or other securities become common shares.

## Quick Comparison

Market cap is usually calculated using current shares outstanding. Fully diluted market cap uses a larger potential share count.

Simple comparison:

1. Market cap: current share price multiplied by current shares outstanding.
2. Fully diluted market cap: current share price multiplied by estimated fully diluted shares.
3. Market cap reflects current issued shares.
4. Fully diluted market cap includes potential shares from dilutive securities.
5. Market cap may look smaller.
6. Fully diluted market cap may show a higher potential valuation.

Both numbers can be useful, but they answer different questions.

## Why It Matters To Traders

This matters because a stock can look cheap based on current market cap but much less cheap when potential dilution is included.

This is where traders can get into trouble. They may compare a stock's market cap to a catalyst without checking how many warrants or convertibles could become shares.

A careful review asks:

1. What are current shares outstanding?
2. What is the current market cap?
3. What securities could become common shares?
4. What is the estimated fully diluted share count?
5. What would the fully diluted market cap be?
6. Are warrants in the money?
7. Are convertibles fixed or variable?
8. Are resale shares registered?

The basic market cap may not tell the full story.

## How Market Cap Works

Market cap is usually calculated by multiplying the stock price by shares outstanding.

For example:

1. Stock price: $2.00.
2. Shares outstanding: 20 million.
3. Market cap: $40 million.

This gives a quick view of the company's current equity value based on current shares.

## How Fully Diluted Market Cap Works

Fully diluted market cap uses an estimate of possible shares after dilutive securities are included.

For example:

1. Stock price: $2.00.
2. Current shares outstanding: 20 million.
3. Potential warrant and convertible shares: 15 million.
4. Fully diluted shares: 35 million.
5. Fully diluted market cap: $70 million.

This does not mean all 35 million shares are trading today. It shows a possible valuation if those securities become shares.

## Example Scenario

A company has 10 million shares outstanding and trades at $3.00. The basic market cap is $30 million. But filings show 20 million possible shares from warrants and preferred stock.

A stronger review asks:

1. Are those warrants exercisable?
2. What are the exercise prices?
3. Are preferred shares convertible?
4. Are there ownership limits?
5. Are the shares registered for resale?
6. Does the diluted market cap change the trade thesis?

This helps traders avoid relying on a simple market cap number.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is ignoring fully diluted shares when reviewing small-cap valuation.

Another mistake is assuming all potential shares are immediately trading. They may not be.

Traders also get into trouble when they ignore variable conversion securities, because the potential share count can change.

A fourth mistake is comparing one company's basic market cap to another company's fully diluted market cap.

## FAQ

### What is market cap?

Market cap is usually calculated by multiplying current stock price by current shares outstanding.

### What is fully diluted market cap?

Fully diluted market cap estimates market value using fully diluted shares, including potential shares from warrants, options, convertibles, or preferred stock.

### Is fully diluted market cap always higher?

It is usually higher when there are dilutive securities, because the share count estimate is larger.

### Does fully diluted market cap mean all shares are trading?

No. It does not mean all potential shares are currently trading. It is an estimate for valuation and dilution review.

### Why do traders review fully diluted market cap?

Traders review it to understand whether a stock's valuation still makes sense after potential share dilution is included.

### Can fully diluted share count change?

Yes. It can change after new offerings, warrant exercises, conversions, equity grants, or changes in security terms.
