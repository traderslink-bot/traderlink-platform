# Building A Playbook From Reviewed Trades

A trading playbook is more useful when it comes from reviewed trades, not from memory, screenshots, or one exciting outcome. The purpose is to turn repeated evidence into clearer setup criteria, risk rules, disqualifiers, and review questions.

This lesson connects Academy education to the habit of building a process over time.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Explain what a trading playbook is.
- Use reviewed trades as evidence.
- Separate setup criteria from disqualifiers.
- Add risk, execution, and behavior rules to a playbook.
- Avoid turning one good outcome into a rule.
- Connect playbook building to Trader Intelligence review.

## Quick Definition

A trading playbook is a documented set of conditions for a setup or trading style.

A useful playbook includes:

- Setup name.
- Market context.
- Required conditions.
- Disqualifying conditions.
- Risk rules.
- Entry and exit planning rules.
- Management rules.
- Common mistake patterns.
- Example trades.
- Review questions.

The playbook should be updated from reviewed trades, not from guesses.

## Why Reviewed Trades Matter

Traders often create rules based on how a trade felt. That can be dangerous. A trade that made money may still have been poorly planned, and a trade that lost money may still have followed a good process.

Reviewed trades give better evidence:

- What the setup looked like before entry.
- Whether risk was defined.
- Whether liquidity was clean.
- Whether the trader followed the plan.
- Whether the trade failed for reviewable reasons.
- Whether the same mistake repeated.

The playbook should come from patterns across trades, not from one chart.

## Playbook Building Process

A practical playbook process:

1. Choose one setup or style.
2. Collect completed trades from that category.
3. Separate planned trades from impulsive trades.
4. Review context, risk, execution, management, and behavior.
5. Identify what the best examples had in common.
6. Identify what the worst examples had in common.
7. Write required conditions.
8. Write disqualifying conditions.
9. Add risk and management rules.
10. Forward test the updated rules before trusting them.

This keeps the playbook grounded in evidence.

## Setup Criteria

Setup criteria describe what must be present before a trade belongs in the playbook.

For example, a pullback playbook might require:

- Clear prior move.
- Pullback into a defined area.
- Volume context.
- Risk level close enough to size properly.
- Liquidity acceptable for entry and exit.
- No major news risk the trader has not planned for.

These are not buy or sell instructions. They are classification rules.

## Disqualifiers

Disqualifiers may be even more important than criteria.

Examples:

- Spread too wide.
- No clear invalidation.
- Stock already extended far from the planned level.
- Volume fading sharply.
- News unclear or unverified.
- Position size would exceed risk limit.
- Trader is taking the trade because of frustration or FOMO.

Disqualifiers protect the playbook from becoming a vague wish list.

## Realistic Example

A trader reviews 30 completed breakout trades. The strongest reviewed examples had clean consolidation, rising volume, a nearby invalidation area, and room before the next resistance zone.

The weakest examples were late entries after the breakout was already extended, often with wider spreads and no clear stop.

The playbook update becomes:

- Required: defined range, clear breakout level, volume expansion, acceptable spread, and planned invalidation.
- Disqualifier: entry far above the breakout level without a risk plan.
- Review rule: tag all late breakout entries separately.

That is a useful playbook improvement because it came from repeated evidence.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is building a playbook from one memorable winner.

Another mistake is writing only setup criteria and skipping disqualifiers.

Traders also make playbooks too broad. "Momentum" is not specific enough by itself.

Another mistake is ignoring execution. A setup may look good on a chart but fail in practice because of spread, slippage, or poor fills.

A final mistake is changing the playbook after every single trade.

## FAQ

### What is a trading playbook?

A trading playbook is a documented set of setup criteria, disqualifiers, risk rules, management rules, and review questions.

### Should a playbook include losing trades?

Yes. Losing trades can show weak conditions, poor execution, disqualifiers, and risk mistakes.

### How many trades do I need before changing a playbook?

There is no perfect number, but changes based on one trade are usually weak. A cleaner sample gives better evidence.

### Is a playbook the same as a trading signal?

No. A playbook organizes reviewable criteria. It does not guarantee a trade will work or tell users what to trade.

### What is a disqualifier?

A disqualifier is a condition that should prevent a trade from fitting the playbook, such as no clear risk level or an unacceptable spread.

### How does Trader Intelligence help?

Trader Intelligence can help organize completed trades and repeated patterns so playbook rules are based on review evidence.
