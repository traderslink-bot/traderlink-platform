# Convertible Notes

This lesson is part of the **Small-Cap Stocks, Float And Dilution** course. It follows [Pre-Funded Warrants](/academy/pre-funded-warrants/) and leads into [Preferred Stock](/academy/preferred-stock/).

The goal is not to make small-cap trading look easy or exciting. The goal is to understand the company, share structure, financing context, liquidity, and risk before treating any move as meaningful.

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Explain what convertible notes are.
- Review conversion terms, fixed versus variable conversion, maturity, interest, and share-count impact.
- Understand why convertibles can create dilution risk when debt can become equity.
- Avoid reacting to convertible debt without reading the actual conversion mechanics.
- Connect convertible-note review to filing context, cash needs, and trade review.

Convertible notes are debt securities that can convert into equity under certain terms. Traders often watch convertible notes because they can affect dilution risk, share count, future supply, and a company's financing structure.

A convertible note is not automatically bullish or bearish. It may provide needed capital, but it can also create risk if the conversion terms are heavily discounted, variable, or tied to a weak share structure. The details matter.

## Quick Definition

A convertible note is a loan or debt instrument that may be converted into shares.

Important terms include:

1. Principal amount.
2. Interest rate.
3. Maturity date.
4. Conversion price.
5. Fixed or variable conversion terms.
6. Discount rate.
7. Default provisions.
8. Conversion caps or floors.
9. Shares issuable on conversion.
10. Registration rights.

For traders, the key question is how many shares the note could become and under what conditions.

## Why It Matters To Traders

Convertible notes matter because they can turn debt into equity. That can change the share count and create potential selling pressure if converted shares enter the market.

This is where traders can get into trouble. A company may announce financing, but the note terms may create future dilution that is not obvious from the headline.

A careful review asks:

1. How much debt was issued?
2. Is the conversion price fixed or variable?
3. Is there a discount to market price?
4. Are there default penalties?
5. Are conversion shares registered?
6. Is there a floor price?
7. How many shares could be issued?
8. Does the company have enough cash to repay instead of converting?

The conversion language is often the most important part.

## How It Works

A company may issue a convertible note to raise capital. The investor lends money, and the note may later convert into shares according to the agreement.

Convertible notes can appear in:

1. Private placements.
2. Debt restructurings.
3. Bridge financings.
4. PIPE deals.
5. Acquisition financing.
6. Distressed financing.
7. Related-party financing.

Some notes are relatively straightforward. Others can be complex and risky, especially if the conversion price floats with the market.

Do not rely on the phrase "convertible note" by itself. Review the actual note agreement, purchase agreement, registration-rights language, and any resale registration statement. Fixed conversion terms, market-price formulas, floors, caps, defaults, and amendments can create very different share-count outcomes.

## Example Scenario

A company issues a $2 million convertible note with a conversion price equal to a discount to the lowest trading price over a recent period.

A stronger review asks:

1. How many shares could conversion create at current prices?
2. Does the conversion price adjust lower if the stock drops?
3. Are there limits on ownership?
4. Are there default penalties?
5. Is the company likely to repay in cash?
6. Are the shares registered for resale?
7. Did the stock already have dilution risk before the note?

This helps traders understand why convertible note details can matter after the initial financing headline.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is treating all convertible notes the same. Fixed conversion notes and variable conversion notes can have very different risk profiles.

Another mistake is ignoring the maturity date. If a note is close to maturity, repayment or conversion pressure may increase.

Traders also get into trouble when they ignore default provisions. Default terms can sometimes make dilution risk worse.

A fourth mistake is not estimating the possible share count impact.

## Review Questions

A structured trade review can help connect convertible note details to trade decisions.

Useful review notes include:

1. Note issuance date.
2. Principal amount.
3. Conversion price or formula.
4. Fixed or variable conversion terms.
5. Maturity date.
6. Default language.
7. Potential shares issuable.
8. Registration status.
9. Stock reaction after note disclosure.
10. Whether the trade considered conversion risk.

Over time, this can help traders avoid ignoring hidden dilution risks.

## FAQ

### What are convertible notes?

Convertible notes are debt securities that can convert into shares under specific terms.

### Are convertible notes dilution?

They can become dilutive if converted into shares. The impact depends on the conversion terms and share price.

### What is a conversion price?

The conversion price is the price used to determine how many shares the note can convert into.

### Are variable conversion notes risky?

They can be riskier because the number of shares issued may increase if the stock price falls, depending on the terms.

### Do convertible notes always convert?

No. Some may be repaid in cash, amended, refinanced, or converted depending on the agreement and company situation.

### What should traders check first?

Traders often check principal amount, conversion terms, maturity, discount, floors, default provisions, registration rights, and potential share count impact.
