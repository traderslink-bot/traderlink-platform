# Premarket Trading

Premarket trading happens before the regular session opens. It can reveal active names, early levels, catalyst reactions, and liquidity conditions before the bell.

The important lesson is preparation. Premarket movement can be useful context, but extended-hours trading can be thin, spread out, and easy to misread.

![Premarket candlestick chart showing premarket high, premarket low, opening bell, volume, catalyst review, and spread check.](/academy/images/chart-reading/premarket-session-workflow.svg)

## Lesson Objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Define premarket trading.
- Understand why liquidity and spreads can be different before the open.
- Map premarket high, premarket low, and key zones.
- Review catalyst quality before reacting to a move.
- Use premarket as preparation, not prediction.

## Quick Definition

Premarket trading is extended-hours trading before the regular market opens.

Traders often watch:

- Premarket high and low.
- News-driven movers.
- Earnings or filing reactions.
- High relative volume.
- Spread and liquidity.
- Early support and resistance.
- Whether the move is extended before regular-hours volume arrives.

Premarket levels are reference areas. They do not guarantee what regular-hours price will do.

Exact premarket access, order types, routing, and available symbols can vary by broker and venue. Treat premarket as a lower-liquidity preparation segment unless your own platform, account rules, and execution constraints are clear.

## How This Fits The Workflow

Premarket is the first filter in the day trading workflow.

- Identify what is active.
- Read the source of the catalyst.
- Check whether volume is meaningful for the time of day.
- Review spread and liquidity.
- Map premarket high and low as zones.
- Decide what would make the idea invalid.
- Prepare for the regular open to change the setup.

Premarket should make the open less chaotic, not more emotional.

## When It Can Mislead

Premarket can mislead when a chart looks clean but the order book is thin. A small amount of volume can create a large candle. A wide spread can make entries and exits harder than the chart suggests.

It can also mislead when traders assume a premarket move will continue after the open. Regular-hours volume can confirm, reject, fade, or completely reshape the early move.

## Realistic Example

A small-cap stock releases news at 7:00 AM and moves from a quiet range into active premarket volume. The premarket high forms near a prior resistance area. The premarket low sits near the first consolidation after the headline.

A prepared trader maps both zones, checks the spread, reads the catalyst, reviews recent filings, and writes down what would make the setup too risky. At the open, the plan is to observe whether regular-hours volume respects or rejects those levels.

That is a workflow. It is not a prediction.

## Common Mistakes

One common mistake is chasing a premarket spike in thin liquidity.

Another mistake is ignoring a wide spread because the candle looks strong.

Traders also make mistakes when they treat low-volume premarket levels as stronger than they are.

Another mistake is reacting to a headline before reading the source.

A final mistake is holding into the open without a plan for changing conditions.

## FAQ

### What is premarket trading?

Premarket trading is extended-hours trading that happens before the regular market session opens.

### Why do traders watch premarket movers?

Traders watch premarket movers to understand early catalyst reactions, volume, levels, liquidity, and possible regular-session context.

### Is premarket trading risky?

Yes. Premarket can have lower liquidity, wider spreads, and sharper moves on fewer shares than regular-hours trading.

### Do premarket levels always matter after the open?

No. Premarket levels are reference areas. Regular-hours volume can respect, ignore, or reshape them.
