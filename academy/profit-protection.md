# Protecting Open Profits

![Protecting Open Profits diagram](/academy/images/risk-management-trade-planning/profit-protection.svg)

For a long position, an unrealized profit is a gain on shares still held. A realized profit comes from shares already sold. A quote showing a gain does not fix the eventual exit price.

## Choose How You Want To Manage The Gain

A position that has risen gives you choices, each with a tradeoff. Selling everything realizes the result on all filled shares and ends further exposure. Selling part realizes some of the gain while leaving the rest able to rise or fall. A trailing exit keeps shares open while moving the exit upward as the chosen market price reaches new highs.

Suppose 200 shares bought at $5.00 now trade near $5.40, the original target. One trader's plan sells all 200 there because the trade was intended to end at that resistance area. If the order fills at $5.40, the price gain is $80.00. They have no remaining shares to benefit from a later rise or lose in a reversal.

Another plan sells 100 there and keeps 100 for a possible continuation, with a defined exit for the remainder. That first fill realizes $40.00. If the rest later exits at $5.60, it gains another $60.00 and the full trade gains $100.00. If it exits at $5.20, it gains another $20.00 and the full trade gains $60.00. Both totals are before costs. The partial exit exchanged some certainty about the current result for continued exposure on fewer shares.

A third plan keeps all shares and trails an exit rather than selling at the original target. It needs a reason for continuing the trade, such as a setup that allows holding through a sustained move. The trailing example below explains how the trigger changes; its trigger level still does not guarantee a fill price.

Choose the approach that fits the intended trade and compare how it performs across comparable completed trades. Do not select the best-looking approach after seeing the entire price path and assume you could have known that outcome in advance.

## Separate The Open And Closed Shares

A trader buys 200 shares at $5.00. Using a current market price of $5.40 to value the shares, their open price gain is 200 × $0.40 = $80.00 before costs. This values the shares at the reference price; it does not guarantee the entire position can sell there.

If 100 shares actually sell at $5.40, the trader realizes $40.00 before costs. The remaining 100 shares have $40.00 of unrealized gain at that same reference price.

| After the partial exit | Amount before costs |
|---|---|
| Realized gain on 100 shares | $40.00 |
| Unrealized gain on 100 remaining shares at $5.40 | $40.00 |

If the remaining shares later sell at $4.90, that exit loses $10.00. The completed trade still gains $30.00 before costs: $40.00 − $10.00.

## A Stop At Entry Is Not Guaranteed Breakeven

Placing a sell stop at the $5.00 entry price can reduce the intended price-loss distance on the remaining shares. A fill at $4.95 still loses $5.00 on 100 shares, and costs remain. Calling the trade “risk free” after moving a stop hides that execution uncertainty.

## A Trailing Stop Follows Rising Prices

A trailing stop changes its trigger using a specified distance or percentage from the market price selected in the order settings, such as the last traded price. The broker's settings determine how it updates and when it works. A trader can also manually raise an exit using new chart levels.

A closer exit may preserve more of an open gain if filled as intended, but normal price movement may trigger it before a later rise. A wider exit leaves more room and more gain exposed. Neither choice guarantees the best exit.

Choose a management rule before the trade where possible. Compare how much was realized, how much remained open and what the eventual fills produced. “I gave back profit” is clearer when the quantities and prices show exactly which gain changed.

## How The Remaining Shares Can Change The Total Profit

After realizing $40.00 on the first 100 shares, the trader still holds 100 shares bought at $5.00. If those remaining shares exit at $4.80, they lose $20.00, leaving the complete trade at +$20.00 before costs. If they exit at $4.40 after a gap, they lose $60.00 and the complete trade finishes at −$20.00 before costs.

A profitable partial exit can cushion a later loss without guaranteeing that the whole trade stays profitable. Keep the remaining quantity and its possible exits visible alongside the realized gain.

## Follow A Trailing Stop Through A Price Move

Suppose a trader manually sets a sell stop $0.20 below the highest traded price reached since starting this trailing rule. At $5.40, the stop is $5.20. A new high of $5.50 raises it to $5.30. If price then falls to $5.45, the stop stays at $5.30; it does not drop to $5.25. Reaching the stop starts the exit process, and the actual fill can differ from $5.30.

A broker trailing order may use a different reference or update method from a line drawn manually on a chart. Verify the actual settings. It is possible to have a chart showing one exit while the active order uses another.

During review, compare the planned management approach with the complete result. The highest unrealized gain is not necessarily a price at which all shares could have sold, so avoid treating every decline from that screen value as an avoidable trading loss.
